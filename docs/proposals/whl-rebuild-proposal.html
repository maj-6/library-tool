1 · Where we stand

▼

A full audit of the catalogue, the files, the reading experience, and the public voice. Every number below was measured, not estimated.

93%core fields present on published books — title, author, year, subject, language, length
26%present for publisher, place, edition and description — where the real gap is
90 MBdownloaded before a single page appears, on our largest volume
487published entries that duplicate a book already catalogued

The catalogue

  • The core of the published catalogue is in better shape than it looks. Across the 4,356 books a visitor can actually reach, the six fields people search on — title, author, year, subject, language, length — are 93% filled, and 77% of books have all six. Any account of this collection should start there.
  • The gap is in the imprint. Publisher is recorded for 69% of published books, a description for 49%, and then it falls away: place of publication 3.5%, subtitle 7%, edition 2%. Averaged across all eleven fields that is the “about a third missing” figure — but the third that is missing is almost entirely this, not the basics.
  • 971 further entries are unpublished drafts, invisible to visitors and much thinner: an author on 18% of them, a year on 31%, a subject on 17%, and 219 with no file attached at all. That is reasonable for a working backlog — but it is a fifth of the catalogue that cannot be published as it stands, and it should be counted separately rather than folded into judgements about the live site.
  • Accuracy is a separate question from completeness, and it is where the harm is. Twelve published books read page by page against their records turned up errors of the kind readers notice: an editor recorded as author; a first-edition date printed on a much later reprint; a year copied from a librarian’s pencilled shelf-mark; a twentieth-century donor recorded as the author of a sixteenth-century manuscript.
  • A filled field is not a checked field. In that same twelve, the title matched the book exactly in three cases and the authors in two. The rest were not wrong so much as partial — volume numbers absorbed into the title, author statements truncated or stripped of their roles. This is the honest qualification of the 93% above: the basics are present, and they are frequently approximate.
  • Subjects were assigned in bulk — botany volumes filed under Ayurveda because a batch was tagged at once. Three of the twelve carried a subject that was simply wrong.
  • 852 published titles — one in five — are volumes of sets or journals the catalogue cannot relate to one another. They belong to about 400 families; the largest are journal runs of nine to twelve issues, each currently standing alone with its volume number jammed into the title text where nothing can sort it.
  • 4,563 published entries describe about 4,075 distinct works — 487 are a second or third scan of a book already catalogued, undetected because no file fingerprint is recorded.
  • 235 published entries carry a broken link; entries with non-Latin characters in their address fail to match their own records. Corrections are keyed to a spreadsheet row number, so inserting a row misfiles every correction after it, with no error to warn you.
  • No rights field exists at all — nothing records who digitised a book, or under what terms we may show it.

Reading and finding

  • Opening a book downloads the entire file first — between 4 and 90 MB. On a 494-page volume, nothing had appeared after thirty seconds of waiting.
  • Text cannot be selected. Search inside a book works only where the file happens to carry a hidden text layer: nine of twelve sampled books have one (uncorrected), three have none, including the Chinese fascicle.
  • No page has an address. Nothing in the collection can be cited, linked, bookmarked, or quoted precisely — the single most consequential gap on this list.
  • Browsing means ten cards a page across 495 pages, with a flat subject list and no filters by author, year, or language, and no sort.
  • A book’s page shows five flat fields; when a field is empty the label still prints, so the page advertises what we do not know.
  • The viewer does not work usefully on a phone, where a growing share of readers arrive.

Voice, trust, and infrastructure

  • The site speaks in a promotional register while our plates and manuscripts — the most persuasive material we own — barely appear.
  • Some descriptions were written from titles alone. One presents a fourteenth-century Italian manuscript as though it were a modern wellness paperback: fluent, confident, and almost entirely untrue.
  • The footer’s “AI Search” is a separate system we do not control, served over an unencrypted connection at a bare numeric address. It shows no authors at all and uses raw filenames as titles — though the missing information exists in our own data and matched fifteen out of fifteen on test.
  • Eighty-eight per cent of our files are compressed derivatives with their origins stripped: renamed, re-saved, and no longer traceable to the institution that digitised them. Only a handful still carry a source number.
  • Our own storage will not release images to a web page (a missing configuration line), so any new reader is blocked until that is fixed — a half-hour job nobody has been assigned.

None of this reflects badly on anyone. It is what happens when a collection grows faster than the tools built to describe it. The collection itself is the rare thing: thousands of digitised volumes across five centuries and a dozen languages, gathered by people who know the material, and given away free. Everything proposed here is in service of that.

The reason the errors matter more than their number suggests: credibility is the only currency a free library earns. A scholar deciding whether to cite us, or a foundation deciding whether to fund us, forms a judgement in about ten seconds — and every visible error spends a little of it.

2 · The case for rebuilding

▼

The honest question is not “are these faults real” — they are measured — but “why rebuild rather than repair”. Five reasons, in the order they convinced us.

  • Every fault above traces to two foundations, not to a hundred bugs. One is the record — a single flat row per book. The other is delivery — a whole file handed over at once. Fix the surface and both remain; fix the foundations and most of the list disappears as a side effect.
  • Repair work done now would have to be redone. Correct a date today and it lands in a field that cannot express [1518?]. Relate a journal run today and there is nowhere to record the relation. Every hour spent improving records inside the current model is an hour that must be spent again after it changes.
  • The one thing only we can offer cannot be built on the present model at all. A plant index requires page addresses, confirmed occurrences, and a name authority. The current site has none of the three, and no amount of patching produces them.
  • Doing nothing is not free. Scans keep arriving into a structure that mis-files them; duplicate rows keep multiplying; rights stay unrecorded while the law changes annually in our favour; and a public AI search we do not control keeps answering in our name.
  • It is far cheaper than it sounds. Page-at-a-time delivery is a solved problem used by every major library; the storage is already paid for; the word positions needed for in-book search are already produced by our text recognition and currently discarded. This is mostly assembly, not invention.
The one-sentence case: we hold a collection of the first rank inside a presentation of the last, and the gap between the two is the opportunity — nobody else can close it, because nobody else has the books.
  • Nothing is thrown away and nothing goes dark. The present site keeps running while the new one fills up book by book; existing entries migrate as they are and improve afterwards as small reviewable tasks.
  • Each phase is useful alone. If work stopped after phase one, the library would still be materially better than it is today. That is the test every phase must pass.

There is a version of this argument that overreaches, and we should avoid it: the claim that the current site is worthless. It is not. It has delivered thousands of books to readers for years, free, and that is not nothing. The argument is narrower and stronger — that the next increment of value is unreachable from here, and that the cost of reaching it grows every month we add material to a structure that cannot hold it.

The comparison worth making is not with other herbal sites; it is with what a reader has learned to expect from any serious library: open a book instantly, cite a page, search inside, trust the record. We are measured against that standard whether or not we choose to be.

3 · Records that fit the books

▼

Today a book is a spreadsheet row: one title, one author, one year, one file. Adequate for a paperback; wrong for nearly everything we hold.

  • Journals and sets become one record with volumes beneath them — not a hundred unrelated entries whose titles have the volume number jammed in.
  • People are recorded with roles: author, editor, translator, illustrator, engraver, printer, former owner. This alone removes a whole class of our current errors.
  • Dates can be honest. [1518?] means we inferred it; a sortable range sits behind the display form, so honesty does not break search.
  • Copies are described as copies — our Woodville’s bound-in manuscript index, our Fuchs’s contemporary hand-colouring. This is what makes our copy worth consulting rather than any copy.
  • Rights status, file fingerprints, outward reference numbers, and a brief record flag so an honest stub is distinguishable from confident emptiness.
  • Every record is versioned and citable; every change — human or machine — arrives as a proposal that a named person approves.

Three examples of why the flat row fails. A journal run: a hundred issues currently look like a hundred unrelated books. A manuscript with no title page: someone invents a title, and the catalogue presents the invention as though the book announced it. An undated book: the field demands a year, so a guess is entered and becomes indistinguishable from a fact.

Migration is incremental. Existing entries move across as they are; improvement becomes a queue of small reviewable tasks rather than an impossible re-cataloguing project.

4 · A reader that respects readers

▼
  • Page-at-a-time delivery: any book opens in about a second, whatever its size.
  • Deep zoom on plates; a permanent link to every page; the full download still available for anyone who wants the whole file.
  • Search inside a book with hits highlighted on the page image — we already produce the word positions during text recognition and currently discard them.
  • Works properly on a phone, which the current viewer effectively does not.
  • One fix comes first: our storage must be allowed to serve images to a web page. Until that half-hour configuration change is made, no new reader can display a single scan.

The scan is kept as page images at several zoom levels; the reader fetches only what is on screen, and more detail only when you zoom. This is the standard approach at essentially every major digital library, so we are adopting a solved solution rather than inventing one.

The same change unlocks the features we lack: addressable pages make citation possible, and word positions make in-book search visual rather than a list of numbers.

5 · Interactivity worth building

▼

Roughly in order of value against effort. Not gimmicks — things scholars, students, and browsing visitors actually ask for.

Working with the text

  • Compare editions side by side — the same plant in Gerard 1597, Culpeper 1653, Woodville 1834, the eclectic dispensatory 1898. The changing description is the history of the subject.
  • Personal shelf — saved books, bookmarked pages with private notes, exportable as a formatted citation list.
  • Highlight and annotate, with a shareable link back to the exact spot — a pencil line and a slip of paper, made citable.
  • Tap a word for a gloss — archaic terms, apothecary symbols, obsolete plant names, always marked as our editorial addition rather than the book’s words.
  • A key to the apothecary symbols (℞, ƶ, ⅘, ana, q.s.) linked from any page that contains them.
  • “How to read this book” primers — a single page explaining what a dispensatory, a receipt book, or a printed herbal actually is, linked from every work of that kind.

Looking at the books

  • Plate gallery — every illustration in the collection, browsable and filterable, downloadable at full resolution.
  • Light-table overlay — lay two plates over one another with a slider, and copying, recutting, and mirroring become visible at a glance.
  • Woodblock tracing — the same figure recurring across six later books is a genuine story this collection can tell, and no one has told it for herbals.
  • Marginalia index — annotations, pressed plants, ownership inscriptions, and pen trials, gathered and page-linked.
  • A page-furniture explainer — a viewer mode that labels catchwords, signatures, foliation and rubrication on the actual page, teaching book history in place.

Finding your way in

  • Curated reading paths — a sequence of pages with commentary: a lesson plan, an exhibition, and a fundraising asset at once.
  • Small exhibitions — eight to twelve items with proper wall-label prose, rotated quarterly, with guest curators later.
  • Open a random page — one button, optionally filtered to illustrated pages. The cheapest good idea on this list.
  • A weekly featured opening — one spread and 150 words, archived so the back-run becomes a browsable trail of its own.
  • A timeline shelf and an imprint map — the collection as five centuries of spines, and as a geography of where these books were printed, both straight from catalogue data.

Taking it away

  • Pocket editions — a clean e-book of a finished translation, every paragraph still carrying its page link.
  • Read-aloud audio for translated text, with a checked pronunciation list for botanical names.
  • A properly accessible reading mode — text-first for screen readers, large print, full keyboard navigation.
  • Later: a crowd-transcription desk, and a “cite this page” widget other sites can embed.

6 · Translations and living editions

▼

Many of our foreign-language holdings have never been translated into English at all — not badly, never. This is the most quotable thing the library could do, and the hardest to sustain, so the machinery matters as much as the ambition.

What the reader sees

  • A three-column reader: page image | transcription | English — each aligned to the others sentence by sentence, so clicking a line lights up its place on the page.
  • Machine drafts; a named human approves passage by passage, and each passage shows its status, so a reader always knows what they are looking at.
  • More than one translation may coexist — a literal rendering and a readable one serve different people, and pretending there is a single right answer is itself a distortion.
  • Uncertainty is printed, not hidden: disputed reading, illegible, conjectural are honest states with a settled look on the page.
  • Living editions — versioned like scholarly editions, correctable in public without erasing history, citable by version, contributors credited by name.
  • Reader corrections enter the same review queue as staff edits — critics become contributors.

The machinery that makes it sustainable

  • A term ledger per tradition — Galenic, alchemical, Ayurvedic, Chinese materia medica each carry an agreed list of renderings, and a draft that departs from one is flagged before a human ever sees it.
  • Translation memory — every approved passage is remembered, so the boilerplate that recurs across thousands of volumes (printers’ privileges, dedications, dosage formulae) is translated once and offered everywhere after.
  • Herbals copy each other heavily — where a passage recurs almost verbatim in a later book, the approved translation arrives as a pre-filled draft marked adapted from, and needs only light review.
  • Changing a term updates the editions. Revise an agreed rendering and every passage that used the old one is queued for a one-click mechanical update, so the ledger and the texts never drift apart.
  • The style guide runs as a check, not a memo — keep original weights and measures with a bracketed modern equivalent; never modernise a disease name — enforced automatically on every draft.
  • A precedent registry: how we render X, and why, searchable, so a new translator learns in one lookup that theriaca stays untranslated by earlier ruling.
  • Two independent renderings for anything dangerous — recipes, doses, preparations — diffed automatically, with disagreements escalated before approval.
  • Occasional back-translation audits: a monthly random sample is machine-translated back into the source language, and the divergent ones return to review as suspected drift.
  • The route is recorded. A translation made through a pivot (Latin → 1546 German → English) says so on the page, and joins a standing list for direct retranslation.
  • Discussion becomes a footnote — when a thread about a hard passage resolves, its conclusion is promoted to a reader-visible translator’s note instead of being buried.

People, credit, and momentum

  • Trial passages, not CVs — applicants translate three calibrated samples scored against reference renderings, and earn badges per language and tradition that decide what they may draft or approve.
  • Every edition names a steward, with a short handover packet of open questions and conventions, so an edition survives the departure of the person who began it.
  • Sampler first — publish a translated contents page and one representative chapter, and let reader interest rank the backlog before committing to a whole volume.
  • Translation sprints — a packaged weekend format (one work, pre-cut passages, glossary loaded, progress board) that reliably leaves an edition measurably nearer its next freeze.
  • An errata feed per edition, and a since your last visit view that highlights what changed, with an old/new toggle.
  • Citations name the version, resolve to exactly the text that was cited, and say plainly when the living edition has since moved on.
  • Later: a printed volume from any frozen release, with contributors in the front matter and the release number in the colophon; and aligned read-aloud audio that is flagged stale by the same machinery when its passage is revised.

This is also the honest answer to the word World in our name. The shelves today are mostly English and mostly Victorian, while extraordinary material sits freely available at major institutions. One Arabic herbal carries plant names in nine languages across some two thousand entries — a translation key in itself, and print-only to this day.

First targets: Egyptian medical papyri, the great Arabic herbals, the Aztec codex, Irish and Ethiopian medical manuscripts, Chinese and Ayurvedic materia medica. A flagship translation from each tradition would make this the place where the world’s herbal knowledge can be read side by side. Nobody has built that.

7 · The plant index

▼

Stop organising knowledge only by book; start organising it by what the books are about. If we do one distinctive thing, this is it.

  • Plant pages — every occurrence of a species across all books and centuries, gathered in one place with each occurrence linked to its page.
  • All the names folded together — gentiana in the 1518 Latin tract, “gencyane” in a medieval English hand, Gentiana lutea on Woodville’s plate, 龍脾 in the Chinese materia medica.
  • Preparation pages — a recipe’s drift across time, e.g. compound tincture of gentian from the 1740s to the 1930s, laid out so the changes are visible without prose.
  • Person pages — an author’s works and the works citing them, doubling as the authority list the catalogue itself uses.
  • Occurrences are found in real text, never inferred; a match counts only once a person confirms it.
  • Build once, reuse everywhere: the comparison view, “Ask”, and most future features stand on this layer.

The distinction that keeps it honest

A word on a page, the thing its author meant, and a modern species are three different things. Collapsing them is the commonest error in this field, and it is how a historical library turns into a source of false botanical claims.

  • No reference index of historical remedy names exists anywhere — not for plants, not for preparations. Projects that need one improvise privately, then abandon it.
  • Publishing ours openly, with the uncertainty intact, is the contribution most likely to be cited, funded, and adopted by others — and the one nobody can build faster than us, because it needs the books.
  • Seeding is cheaper than it sounds: one printed nine-language glossary of some two thousand entries, plus a free automated pass that finds botanical names in text we already hold.
  • Later: publish the index as data others can match their own terms against, and as printed cross-references to the great botanical authorities.

Someone asking about gentian would see five hundred years of answers, each linked to the page it came from. That is the difference between a search engine and a reference work — and no institution has built the latter for herbal medicine.

8 · The entity layer in detail

▼

The technical companion to the previous section, for whoever builds this. The plant index is the visible product; the entity layer is the machinery underneath it, and almost every difficult decision in the whole project lives here.

8.1  What has to be represented

Four distinct things, routinely collapsed into one by systems that later cannot be corrected.

  • Name form — a lexical string with its language, script, and period: gencyane, gentiana, Enzian, 龍脾. A name form is not attached to any one book; it is the vocabulary the corpus uses.
  • Mention — one occurrence of a name form at one place in one witness: this page, these coordinates, this character range, in this revision of the transcription.
  • Historical concept — the drug, simple, or preparation an author appears to mean, scoped to a tradition, a period, and a region. The gentiana of a first-century Greek text and the gentian of an 1898 eclectic dispensatory are two concepts, however the modern identification falls out.
  • Modern referent — a present-day taxon, a chemical substance, a material, or the honest value unresolved. This is a target of interpretation, never a property of the text.
  • The same treatment extends beyond plants to preparation (decoction, tincture, electuary), process (drying, calcining, distilling), ailment and body part, measure and unit, person, place, quoted work, and illustration — an illustration is a mention whose anchor is a region with no text at all.
  • A recipe is a structure, not a bag of entities. An ingredient mention carries a quantity, a plant part, a preparation, and an order of operations; storing only “this page mentions gentian” throws away most of what the page says.

8.2  Everything is an assertion

  • Model every link as a row in its own right — subject, predicate, object, plus author, evidence, confidence, review state, created-at, and superseded-by. A foreign key from mention to concept cannot hold any of that, and retrofitting it later means rewriting the whole layer.
  • Nothing is deleted. A rejected identification is marked superseded and stays readable; that record is what lets a later editor see the argument rather than repeat it.
  • Confidence is a small controlled vocabulary, not a float. Certain, likely, possible, disputed, unresolved. Invented decimals imply a precision nobody can defend, and reviewers cannot apply them consistently.
  • Competing assertions coexist. The interface picks a default by rank and review state; the data never collapses to one answer. “Two specialists disagree” is a state the schema must be able to express.
  • Machine output enters as an assertion by a named model version, in state proposed, and is never promoted by another machine.

8.3  Anchoring: how a mention stays attached

Transcriptions get corrected. An anchor that depends only on character positions breaks on the first fix, and a layer that loses its anchors loses everything.

8.4  Storage: relational first

  • A graph database is not required and should not be the starting point. The queries this layer actually serves are two and three hops deep — mention to concept to referent, or concept to mentions to works — which is ordinary relational work. Variable-length path traversal over millions of edges is the case that justifies a graph engine, and it is not a case WHL has yet.
  • One database, with constraints. The workbench needs transactions, uniqueness, and referential integrity far more than it needs exotic traversal. Adding a second engine doubles the operational surface for a project run by very few people.
  • Full-text and fuzzy matching belong in the same database: a trigram index over name forms answers “what is this string close to” without a separate service.
  • The public pages are built, not queried. Entity pages are generated artefacts written out at publish time, so read performance never depends on the database. It serves the workbench and the build, nothing else.

8.5  Finding candidates: matching before models

  • Start with a dictionary, not a model. Exact and normalised matching against a curated name list is cheap, fully auditable, and reproducible — three properties a scholarly layer needs and a language model does not offer. Seed the list from the printed multilingual glossary and from a free automated pass that recognises botanical names in existing text.
  • Then widen recall deliberately. Fuzzy string distance for spelling variation, phonetic keys for names transmitted by ear, and a model pass last, for the cases the list cannot anticipate. Record which method proposed each candidate; it is the only way to know later where recall is coming from.
  • Historical spelling and recognition error are different problems and must not be merged. Gencyane is a period spelling; gcncyane is a misread c for e. Blur them and every recognition fault becomes a permanent lexicographical claim. Where our own text is uncertain, the mention should record that the underlying reading is provisional.
  • Normalisation is per language and period, and it is lossy. Long ſ to s, u/v and i/j interchange, ligature expansion, Arabic and Hebrew vowel pointing, transliteration schemes: all defensible, all destructive. Keep the original string in the mention and the normalised key beside it.
  • Candidates are proposals with a picture attached. Every one enters the review queue carrying the image crop, so a reviewer confirms against the page rather than against our transcription of it.

8.6  Telling concepts apart

  • The same string denotes different things across traditions and centuries; a single lookup table produces confident anachronism. Disambiguation needs the passage’s own context: the language, the date of the witness, the section of the book, the ailment being treated, and above all the co-occurring ingredients, which are the strongest available signal in recipe literature.
  • Do not let identity travel. If A is likely B, and B is likely C, it does not follow that A is C. Chaining likelihoods is how a plausible guess becomes a citable falsehood two hops from its evidence; the schema should refuse to compute transitive identity at all.
  • Resist popularity. Both dictionary matching and language models drift toward the best-known taxon. Measure this directly: if the distribution of identifications is far narrower than the botanical literature for the period, the layer is guessing.
  • A concept may deserve to exist with no modern referent at all — a compound whose composition is disputed, or a name whose plant is genuinely unknown. Those pages are among the most useful the index can publish, because they are where the open questions are.

8.7  External authorities: link, never absorb

  • Store the authority’s own identifier, the date retrieved, and a cached display label — nothing more. Copying their data makes us responsible for keeping it current, which we cannot do.
  • Modern taxonomic synonymy does not transfer to historical identity. That two names are synonyms today says nothing about what a sixteenth-century author meant. Keep the two relationships in separate columns and never join them by accident.
  • Taxonomy changes; assertions must be re-validated by a person, never re-pointed on our behalf. Record which snapshot an assertion was made against, and let a scheduled check raise the ones whose target has moved for a person to look at.
  • Some useful sources — a medicinal plant names service in particular — are available only by arrangement. That conversation should start early, because it shapes what the layer can link to.

8.8  Publishing it as infrastructure

  • A stable, opaque identifier per concept, resolvable as a web address, which never encodes the name. Names change; identifiers must not.
  • A reconciliation service: send a historical string with optional language and date, receive ranked candidate concepts with scores and evidence links. This is the single feature that turns the index from a set of pages into something other projects build on, and it is a modest amount of work once the tables exist.
  • Bulk exports under an open licence, versioned and checksummed: one file of concepts, one of name forms, one of assertions with their evidence. Publishing the assertions rather than the conclusions is what makes the dataset citable.
  • Crosswalk files mapping our concepts to each external authority, one per target, regenerated on release — interoperability without adopting anybody’s ontology.
  • Every published page and export states its release version, so a citation made today still resolves to what it said today.

8.9  Measuring it honestly

  • Measure the stages separately: did we find the mention, was the right concept among the candidates, did the top-ranked assertion turn out correct, and was the review decision itself sound.
  • Candidate recall matters more than top-one accuracy. A reviewer can pick the right answer from five; nobody can pick an answer that was never proposed.
  • Score the preservation of ambiguity. A system that always names one taxon will beat an honest one on naive accuracy while being worthless to a scholar. Where the gold set says unresolved, confidently resolving it is a failure and must be counted as one.
  • Build the gold set from published scholarship per tradition, and never let the same model that does the extraction also write the answers it is graded against.

8.11  Scale, and what to build first

  • The numbers are small for a database and large for people. At roughly 1.3 million pages and a handful of mentions per page, expect single-digit millions of mentions — unremarkable for one ordinary database server, and far beyond what anyone will review. The binding constraint is reviewer attention, so every design choice should be judged by how much of it the feature consumes.
  • Build in this order. Name forms and mentions with their anchors, on one substance, in one tradition; then the review queue with image crops; then concepts with scope; then external links; then the reconciliation service and exports. Each step is useful on its own, and the first two are what everything else is measured against.
  • The pilot is the schema test. One substance traced across languages and centuries will exercise scoping, competing assertions, unresolved cases, and re-anchoring within a few hundred mentions — cheaply enough that getting the model wrong is survivable.

A note on why this section is longer and drier than the rest of the document. Most of what is proposed elsewhere can be revised later at moderate cost: a reader can be redesigned, a page rebuilt, a translation improved. The entity layer is the one part where an early shortcut becomes permanent — if links are stored as plain foreign keys with no author or evidence, then a year of accumulated identifications cannot be argued with, audited, or undone, and the only remedy is to discard them.

The corresponding reassurance is that none of this is exotic. It is a handful of ordinary tables, one careful rule about what an arrow means, and the discipline to keep interpretation out of the text layer.

9 · Books we cannot publish

▼

The line the courts have drawn is between information about a book (permitted) and a substitute for reading it (not). Three visible tiers, each with a plain-English reason printed on the page.

Open

Public domain: read online, download, reuse freely. The great majority of the collection.

Search only

In copyright: full record, plus search that reports where a term appears without showing the text. Upheld by the courts; makes us a finding aid and sends readers to buy or borrow.

Catalogue only

Preserved and described, nothing displayed — used where rights are unresolved, with a clear contact route for rights-holders.

  • A documented rights decision per volume, storing the rule it was decided under, so a yearly job can list the works graduating into the open tier each January.
  • Strict caps so no series of queries could reconstruct a work; a published takedown process and a registered contact.
  • Transparency here is a trust-builder, not an embarrassment — courts have explicitly credited that kind of good-faith apparatus.

Ways to widen the open tier, lawfully

  • Check renewals. A great many mid-century books were never renewed and are already public domain; the registers are public and searchable, and we already hold the dataset needed to do this in bulk.
  • Government publications — agricultural bulletins, extension pamphlets, official pharmacopoeias — are frequently free of copyright regardless of date.
  • The last-twenty-years provision lets a library display a work in the final stretch of its term after a documented check that it is not commercially available. It is genuinely underused.
  • Ask. A one-page letter to estates and defunct publishers of long-out-of-print herbals, with every answer recorded on the record. The cost is postage.
  • Facts are not copyrighted. Even for a dark work we may publish where a plant is mentioned, what a recipe’s ingredients are, and how the book is structured — the index can know about a book it may not show.
  • Terms differ by country. Recording both source-country and local status turns a single “no” into an accurate map of where a work is already free.
  • A public-domain-day queue — scans staged to go live each first of January, turning expiry into a small annual occasion.

10 · “Ask the Library”

▼
The principle: the library answers with pages, not opinions.
  • A short synthesis, then the actual passages — quoted, attributed to book, volume and page, each linked to the scan and highlighted where the words sit.
  • Cross-language matching through the plant index, with the mechanism stated, so gentian finds gentiana and 龍脾 and the reader knows why.
  • Explicit abstention: “the library is silent on this” rather than improvisation. This is a feature, and it should be visibly common.
  • Two manners, one engine. A researcher’s mode citing everything and showing disagreement; a general mode that explains first and keeps the sources one tap away.
  • For in-copyright books: citation, location, at most a brief attributed quotation, and a link to obtain the book.
  • Never health advice — our founding rule, preserve and educate, never prescribe, binds the software too: no dose conversions, no modern claims, and a flag on toxic plants.

Done this way it is not a chatbot bolted onto a library. It is a research assistant that can read five centuries of material in a dozen languages and always shows its work.

One caution we should state publicly rather than discover: a citation proves the sentence matches our transcription of the page, and our transcription can be wrong. That is exactly why the citation opens the image — the scan, not the text, is the evidence of last resort.

11 · AI — the decision we need

▼
  • Appropriate: text recognition, translation drafting, entity extraction into review queues, error flagging, retrieval — all behind human review. This is what lets a handful of colleagues work at institutional scale.
  • Not appropriate: generated content presented as description or scholarship. Some current descriptions were written from titles alone; one describes a fourteenth-century Italian manuscript as though it were a modern wellness paperback — fluent, and almost entirely untrue.
  • Rule 1 — AI may draft; only named people publish.
  • Rule 2 — nothing speaks for the library that cannot cite a page; silence is stated, not filled.
  • Rule 3 — AI is a cost of care, not a revenue strategy. The library stays free, unadvertised, and unsold.

What we already know goes wrong

  • Blank pages produce confident nonsense. Our current recognition invents text on every blank leaf — mathematics, boilerplate in the wrong language, internal debris. Cheap filters must run before any of it is indexed.
  • Fluent fabrication on damaged pages is worse than failure, because it reads correctly. Degraded material must be quarantined for human eyes, not passed through.
  • Silent tidying is a form of error — printed typos corrected, struck-out words flattened into contradictions. A faithful transcription must preserve what is actually there.
  • An empty page once produced a complete fabricated record in a related system. Every extraction step needs the right to say nothing here, and to be believed when it does.
  • Non-Latin scripts are far weaker — roughly half the characters wrong on Chinese title pages. Any translation programme must name a tested recognition path per script, or plan for human transcription from the start.

One medievalist finding that manuscript description learns not that one entry is wrong, but that nothing here can be trusted unchecked. Fluent falsehood is the fastest way to lose the credibility everything else depends on.

Handled properly, though, AI is not a corner cut. It is the reason a small team can meet an institution’s standards while those standards remain entirely human.

12 · The workbench behind the site

▼

The public site is the easy half. What decides whether this survives contact with real work is the editing surface: how a change is proposed, reviewed, published, and undone. Much of this exists already in the desktop cataloguing tools and needs promoting, not inventing.

Editing safely

  • Named changesets — a bulk edit previews every affected record, applies as one named bundle, and reverts in a single click. A bad sweep across 400 records is one undo, not four hundred.
  • Field-level provenance — hover any value to see where it came from: which page of the scan, which dealer record, which named human, with a link to jump there.
  • Preview builds per proposal — reviewers judge the rendered page at a private address, not a raw list of field changes.
  • Cross-layer flags — approving a transcription change automatically marks the translations that depend on it for re-review, with the exact difference attached.
  • A build that refuses to ship bad work — any page containing material below that section’s required review level simply does not publish. Machine drafts cannot leak through a template mistake.

Publishing and prose

  • A block kit for exhibitions — plate-with-caption, timeline entry, quotation-with-citation, compare-two-editions — assembled by choosing catalogue entries rather than writing free-form markup, so every citation is real and checkable.
  • A house-style check on our own prose — flags prescriptive medical phrasing (treats, cures, dosages) at save time, enforcing preserve and educate, never prescribe mechanically rather than by memory.
  • Scheduled publication — an approved change can simply carry a go-live date; no separate scheduling system is needed.
  • A calendar seeded from the catalogue — anniversaries, author dates, and seasonal plants suggest features months ahead, so the front page never depends on remembering.
  • A generated “what improved” feed, built from approved changes with cosmetic edits filtered out — proof the library is alive rather than a static shelf.

Keeping the site honest

  • A pre-publish crawl that checks every internal link, image and citation anchor resolves, and fails loudly instead of shipping quiet dead ends.
  • A redirect ledger — merging or renaming a work writes a permanent redirect automatically, so no address we ever published returns nothing. Given 235 already-broken links, this is not hypothetical.
  • A staleness dashboard — records ranked by how long since a human verified them, weighted by how often readers actually land there.
  • A coverage grid — works by stage (scanned, recognised, transcribed, reviewed, translated), making the long tail visible instead of theoretical.
  • Plain-English change digests — the history rendered nightly as readable sentences (“imprint date corrected from title-page evidence, p. 3”), so a week of changes can be skimmed over coffee.
  • Template fixtures — every page design rendered against our worst records (longest title, no author, mixed scripts, no plates) before a real one finds the breakage.
  • An orphan report — files with no record, records with no files, pages nothing links to.

13 · Adding new books

▼

Every defect in section one entered through an intake that asked too little. The remedy is a single guided path from arrival to live record, with hard stops where policy matters — and it reuses tools we already have, including the phone capture app and the box labels.

  • A fingerprint check before anything else — a file already held becomes another copy of a known work, not a new entry. This attacks the 487 duplicate published entries — and the 256 more sitting in draft — at the source.
  • An accession number minted on arrival, before cataloguing, so nothing can sit in storage untracked.
  • An intake wizard with hard stops — it will not advance while rights status or source is blank. Policy becomes the shape of the form.
  • Bulk pre-fill by external catalogue number, always landing in the review queue and never publishing unattended — outside metadata is often poor and must be hand-picked.
  • A guided merge when a twin is found: the new scan attaches under the existing work as a copy, with its own notes, rather than a rewrite.
  • A scan-quality check before recognition — resolution, blur, skew, page count — so we never spend recognition effort on a scan that must be redone.
  • A standard five-shot condition record (binding, spine, title page, worst damage, text block) taken with the phone app we already have, stored as evidence about the copy.
  • Box labels that report progress — scanning a box shows each book’s stage in place, extending the QR feature already shipped from what is here to how far along is it.
  • A public per-book progress page (received → scanned → recognised → drafted → reviewed → live) — the internal worklist and the donor’s view are the same page.
  • A provisional shelf record — accession number, fingerprint, one photograph, clearly marked unverified — so the backlog is visible and referenceable without lowering the catalogue’s standard. The 971 entries already sitting in draft are the first candidates: most need an author, a year and a subject before they can go live, and a queue that shows exactly which field is missing turns that from a vague backlog into a countable job.
  • A priority score for the queue: rarity, rights openness, gaps in the plant index, reader requests — recomputed nightly, instead of first-in-first-out.
  • A re-recognition campaign manager — batch re-runs write only into the machine layer and produce a difference report; human-approved readings are never overwritten.
  • A wants list, published — the editions we seek, with condition notes, so donors and partner collections can match their shelves against ours.
  • A deed-of-gift flow for donations, binding the gift record to the book’s provenance and crediting the donor by name on the live record if they wish.
  • Partner scans accepted only with a manifest — scanner, date, source shelf-mark, licence — preserved verbatim as copy-level provenance.
  • A weekly orphan sweep: files with no record, records with no file. Intake mistakes surface in days, not at the next migration.

14 · The roadmap

▼

Each phase ships something usable on its own — no big bang, no gamble. If work stopped at the end of any phase, the library would still be better than it is today. Select a phase to see what lands.

What we deliberately do not do first

  • A volunteer platform before there are volunteers. The first ten contributors need a queue and a named reviewer, not a governance system.
  • A finished translation before a sampler. One chapter, published, tells us more about demand than a year of committee.
  • Perfect records before any records. Migration first, improvement as reviewable tasks after — otherwise nothing ships.
  • An assistant before an index. Ask over a collection with no page addresses is a demonstration, not a service.

A note on cost, since it is usually the unasked question. The heavy machine work — recognising, extracting, summarising across the whole collection — is a one-off in the low thousands, not a subscription. Serving questions afterwards is tens of dollars a month at any traffic this library is likely to see. The real budget is human: reviewing, translating, and deciding. That is why every proposal above is judged first by how much reviewer time it consumes.

15 · Keeping it alive

▼

A library that promises permanent citations is making a promise measured in decades. These are cheap now and impossible to retrofit.

  • Deposit every frozen release with an independent archive that issues permanent identifiers. If this project ended tomorrow, the citations would still resolve. Nothing else on this page buys as much credibility for as little.
  • Three copies, two kinds of storage, one off-site — with fingerprints checked on a schedule rather than assumed.
  • A static fallback: the whole public site can render to plain files, so it can outlive its servers, its software, and its budget.
  • Name the body that holds the rights before asking any contributor to sign anything, and license our own work so a successor could carry it on without hunting for permission.
  • Publish the annual keep-alive cost, with a fund that could cover a decade. A concrete number is more persuasive than any statement of intent.
  • Record contributors by a stable internal key, with names attached separately, so credit can be shown, changed, or withdrawn without rewriting history.
